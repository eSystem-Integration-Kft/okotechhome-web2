How it works

If you understand one concept — activated sludge — everything else follows: aeration, chemical sensitivity, the load limits and sludge handling alike.

The process

What happens to the wastewater?

The path of the wastewater from the house, inlet to outlet. The exact number of chambers and their arrangement differ from model to model.

  • 01

    Inlet and mechanical pre-treatment. The wastewater arrives by gravity. In the first chamber the coarser solids settle and the breakdown begins.

  • 02

    Aerated chamber — this is where the treatment happens. A membrane blower introduces fine bubbles into the water. Supplied with oxygen, the bacteria — the activated sludge — break down the organic matter.

  • 03

    Settlement. After aeration the water comes to rest and the sludge settles. Clear water is left on top, the denser sludge layer below.

  • 04

    Sludge return. Part of the settled sludge returns to the aerated chamber: this is what sustains the bacterial community.

  • 05

    Surplus sludge removal. The bacteria multiply, so a surplus is produced continuously. Unless it is drawn off, the system silts up. Handling this is one of the A.B.Clear's distinguishing features.

  • 06

    Treated water outlet. The treated water leaves the unit — and from here the question of water disposal follows, which is a subject in itself.

Activated sludge

What everything else follows from

„Activated sludge” is not dirt but a living mass of bacteria — this is what does the cleaning. Understand that one concept and everything else follows.

Why is aerationneeded? Because these bacteria work with oxygen. Why is the system sensitive to chemicals ? Because disinfectant kills them. Why is sustained underloadinga problem? Because they starve. Why is overloading? Because they cannot keep up. And why is sludge handling? Because they multiply, and the excess has to be drawn off.

The condition of the activated sludge is therefore not a technical curiosity but the measure of the system's health — that is what the settlement (sludge) test measures, and it has to be carried out regularly as part of operation.

Two outputs

What it does sokan not tudnak

Treated water

  • Roughly as much as the wastewater that came in
  • Its quality can be measured: COD, BOD5, suspended solids, nitrogen, phosphorus
  • It is not drinking water, and without further treatment it will not become so
  • It has to be disposed of — a design task in its own right
  • Water disposal is a precondition of the project, not an accessory to it

Surplus sludge

  • Produced continuously as the bacteria multiply
  • Unless it is drawn off, the system silts up
  • Traditionally this is removed by emptying
  • The A.B.Clear uses a sludge bag for this
  • This does not remove the task — it gives a different model for it

The figures

What do the water quality parameters mean?

At the end of the process treated water leaves the unit. The wording matters here: this is not „clean water” in any general sense, and certainly not drinking water How clean it is, is decided not by the eye but by measured parameters.

COD — chemical oxygen demand
Put simply, it shows numerically how much oxygen-demanding pollutant load remains in the water. The lower it is, the less degradable pollution is left.
BOD₅ — biochemical oxygen demand
It measures the same load, but the biologically degradable narrowed to the part, over five days. Together the two show what the biology accomplished and what was left after it.
Suspended solids
It shows mennyi solid, suspended matter remained in the water — that is, how well the activated sludge and the water separated during settlement.
Nitrogen and phosphorus
Nutrients that burden the receiving water. Testing may cover these too; which of them matters on a given project is decided by how the treated water is disposed of.

This says far more than that the outlet water looks clear looks clear. The specific discharge values are worth reading with their measurement conditions — they are in the unit's technical data sheet: A.B.Clear technical data.

Automation does not equal unattended operation

The unit's controller is automatic: it handles the aeration cycles, the sludge return and the phase changes. That does not, however, make the system run by itself indefinitely.

The operating manual prescribes regular tasks: checking the aeration, testing the condition of the activated sludge, checking the compressor, keeping an operating log and periodic maintenance. These are not optional and they are not service tasks — a substantial part of them belongs to the owner.

This is worth knowing before buying, not after. On the operation page we work through item by item what really is the owner's business.

Frequently asked questions

What we are asked most often

How long before the treatment gets going?

Establishing the bacterial community takes time — the system does not reach its usual outlet water quality from the first day after commissioning. The exact period depends on the load and the temperature, so the commissioning documentation records it.

What is a sludge settlement test?

A simple settlement test: in a sample taken into a measuring cylinder we look at how long the sludge takes to settle and in what proportion. This shows the condition of the activated sludge — an owner's task, and a few minutes' work.

Does it work in winter too?

Yes. The unit sits in the ground, which is favourable for temperature, and the biological process generates heat too. In colder weather the process slows but does not stop — provided the load is continuous.
