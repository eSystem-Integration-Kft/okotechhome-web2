Soil and infiltration capacity

Calling the soil „sandy” or „clayey” is not enough on its own. The question is how fast the soil on site can take water — and that has to be measured, not named.

The most important difference

The name of the soil is not a sizing figure

Calling the soil „sandy”, „clayey” or „cohesive” is not enough on its own to size an infiltration system. The substantive question is how at what rate and under what conditions the soil on site can accept water — that is infiltration capacity, and it has to be measured, not named.

The soil of two neighbouring plots can both be called sandy while behaving quite differently because of their stratification, their compaction or an intervening clay layer. That is why Government Decree 147/2010 makes the characteristics of the soil one of the basic conditions for sizing a standalone system — not the name of the soil.

Adequate infiltration capacity is not enough on its own either: the groundwater, the area available, the quantity of treated water and the environmental restrictions together determine the means of disposal.

What can be found out

Where can information about the soil come from?

The sources are not equivalent. The order below roughly follows their reliability — from the bottom up.

  • 01

    The experience of neighbours or of the area. A useful preliminary signal but not measured data: the layering can differ from plot to plot. Good for pre-screening, not for sizing.

  • 02

    Observing earlier excavation. Footings, a pool, a garden pit — these say a great deal about the layers and can be documented with photographs. They do not, however, measure the capacity to accept water.

  • 03

    An earlier geotechnical report. If one was prepared for the building's design, it is often available. Its date must be checked, and whether it covers the soakaway's intended position.

  • 04

    Percolation test. This measures directly what sizing needs: the soil's actual capacity to accept water, at the soakaway's intended position and depth.

What the soil affects

Two separate effects

The soil's type and layering affect…

  • the difficulty and cost of the earthworks
  • the stability of the excavation and whether shoring is needed
  • the quality of the backfill around the tank
  • on made ground, the bearing capacity and settlement
  • whether a geotechnical report is needed

The MEASURED infiltration capacity affects…

  • whether the water can be disposed of on site at all
  • the surface area and volume the soakaway requires
  • whether that area fits within the free space available
  • long-term reliability — silting up, backing up
  • whether another route for water disposal has to be examined

What has to be treated separately

Made ground and layered soil

A Made ground is a separate case: the material, age, compaction and thickness of the fill may be unknown, and the impression at the surface is misleading. On made ground neither bearing capacity nor infiltration can be estimated from the soil type — here testing is not optional.

A Layered soil likewise: beneath a freely draining upper layer there may be a sealing clay layer that holds the water. The soakaway then behaves quite differently at its intended depth than the surface promises. This is why the test has to be carried out at the soakaway's intended position and depth, not at some arbitrary point on the plot.

The result

Four possible outcomes — not „good” or „bad” soil

  • 01

    Indicative data is available. There is information, but it is an estimate or an old document. Enough for pre-screening, not for sizing.

  • 02

    Measurement is needed. The existing figure is insufficient or does not relate to where the soakaway will be. A percolation test follows.

  • 03

    Infiltration can be designed. On the measured value, on-site disposal is realistic; the area required can be calculated together with the daily volume of water.

  • 04

    An alternative means of disposal has to be examined. The soil will not take the volume required, or the area required will not fit. Another means of disposal then has to be examined.

Next step

Without the groundwater, the soil data is not enough either

Even soil that takes water well is not enough if the seasonal groundwater rises to the level of the soakaway, or if a water-protection restriction applies. The two figures have to be looked at together.

Frequently asked questions

What we are asked most often

Is it enough that water drains well next door?

It serves as a preliminary signal but is not enough for sizing. Layering can vary even within a plot, and the neighbour's soakaway may work at a different depth under a different load. The test has to be carried out on your own plot, at the intended position and depth.

What exactly does a percolation test measure?

How fast the soil absorbs water at the intended depth. Together with the daily volume to be disposed of, this value gives the infiltration area required. It cannot be derived from a soil-type label.

I already have a geotechnical report for the building work. Is that enough?

It is often a good starting point, but two things have to be checked: when it was prepared, and which point of the plot and what depth it covers. An investigation made for the foundations was not necessarily carried out at the soakaway's intended position and depth.

Is on-site disposal impossible in clay soil?

Not automatically, but it is harder and needs a larger area — and in some places it cannot be solved. Measurement decides this, not the name of the soil. Where on-site disposal cannot be achieved, another route has to be examined.
