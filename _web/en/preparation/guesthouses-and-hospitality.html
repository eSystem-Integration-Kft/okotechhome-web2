Guesthouses and hospitality

A twenty-bed guesthouse may run at eight guests on annual average and twenty on three peak weekends. The two figures call for different systems.

Bed count is not load

What it is really made of

In sizing a guesthouse or a hospitality business, the nominal bed count is not the same as the treatment unit's actual load. A twenty-bed guesthouse may run at an annual average of eight guests with twenty on three peak weekends — the two call for quite different systems.

Alongside bed spaces, the load profile therefore needs the annual occupancy rate, the peak periods, the opening hours, the staff, and the water use of the laundry, the wellness facilities and any catering.

Two separate wastewater streams

What it does not szabad egybevenni

Domestic — the guests' wastewater

  • Bathroom, WC, handbasin
  • Its composition resembles household wastewater
  • It varies in proportion to the occupancy rate
  • With hydraulic peaks morning and evening
  • This is the task of ordinary biological treatment

Kitchen — the water used in catering

  • Washing up, preparation, cleaning
  • A higher organic, fat and oil load
  • Proportional not to the number of guests but to the number of meals
  • With short, intense peaks following the kitchen's hours
  • It may need PRE-TREATMENT — a grease trap

What you need to provide

The accommodation load profile

  • 01

    Bed count and average annual occupancy. Together the two give a realistic average; the bed count alone shows the maximum.

  • 02

    Peak periods. When it is full, for how long, and how many times a year. At a guesthouse on Lake Balaton this is a few weeks; in a city it follows events.

  • 03

    Days of opening. Open all year or seasonally. If seasonally, the seasonal use page is relevant too.

  • 04

    Number of staff. They too represent a load, typically for the whole of the opening hours.

  • 05

    Catering. Breakfast only, or a full restaurant. The number of meals a day and the kitchen's operating hours.

  • 06

    Laundry. Laundry done on site or by an outside service. An in-house laundry is a substantial, concentrated water use.

  • 07

    Wellness facilities. Pool, jacuzzi, sauna. Changing the pool water is a separate, large one-off volume.

  • 08

    Events. A wedding, a conference — a short, very large peak that does not come from the residential guests.

The kitchen is a separate question

It cannot be solved by “guests × litres”

The fat and oil load of catering wastewater differs from household wastewater, and it directly affects how the biological stage works. At higher kitchen loads the technology can be supplemented with a grease trap — but when that is needed has to be derived from the actual operation.

For wastewater arising from production and service activities, Government Decree 220/2004 treats process and operating data as relevant inputs too. In practice this means a project with a commercial kitchen cannot be sized by a simple „covers × litres per head” method.

Where the kitchen load is significant, the questions of laboratory data and pre-treatment enter early — worth reckoning with rather than meeting at the end of the project.

Next step

For institutions the logic differs again

For a school, a nursery, an office or another community institution, what governs alongside the nominal headcount is the hours of attendance and the operating days per year.

Frequently asked questions

What we are asked most often

My guesthouse has twenty beds. Do I need a twenty-person system?

Not necessarily — probably not. The bed count shows the maximum, but sizing starts from the average occupancy and the regular peaks. Give us the annual occupancy and the number of peak weekends, and a considerably more accurate picture comes together from that.

We have a restaurant too. What does that change?

A great deal. The grease and organic load of kitchen wastewater differ from household effluent, and it may require pre-treatment — typically grease separation. Sizing needs the number of meals a day and the kitchen's operating hours, not the guest nights.

We hold events as well. Should we size for those?

An event is a short, very large peak, and it is typically not worth handling with the size of the unit — that would mean over-sizing on every other day of the year. Give us the frequency and the scale, and we will look at the right approach in that case.

Is a laboratory analysis needed?

At an accommodation business the domestic-type load can usually be estimated well. Where there is a significant kitchen or other process load as well, laboratory data is often unavoidable — without it the sizing would rest on assumption.
